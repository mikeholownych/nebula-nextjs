## Leak 1: Your headline describes what you do, not what changes for them

**What it looks like:**

> "AI-powered project management for modern teams."
> "The fastest way to build landing pages."
> "Compliance software for financial services."

**Why it leaks:**
A stranger arrives from your ad. They already know what you do — that's why they clicked. What they don't know is what happens to *them* if they stay. The headline has to answer that question in four seconds or they leave.

**The pass condition:**
A stranger who has never heard of your product reads the headline and can say: "I understand what I get, and it sounds like it's for someone in my situation."

**Quick test:**
Cover your logo and show the page to someone outside your industry for five seconds. Ask them: "What does this do for you?" If they struggle, your headline is describing the product, not the outcome.

## Leak 2: Your ad made a promise your page doesn't keep

**What it looks like:**

Ad: *"Stop wasting money on ads that don't convert"*
Page headline: *"Welcome to [Product]. The smarter way to grow."*

**Why it leaks:**
The click happens because of the ad's promise. The page is where that promise either gets honored or broken. When they don't match, the visitor's brain registers a discontinuity — and discontinuity reads as distrust. They leave, and your ad spend is gone.

**The pass condition:**
The language, tone, and specific claim in your ad headline appears — or is directly echoed — within the first viewport of the landing page.

**Quick test:**
Open your ad in one tab and your landing page in another. Screenshot both above-the-fold sections. Are they telling the same story? Can you draw a line from the ad's promise to the page's headline?

## Leak 3: You have no proof that it worked for anyone else

**What it looks like:**
The page makes strong claims. There are no quotes, no names, no numbers, no case studies. Maybe there's a generic "Join 500+ companies" badge with no company names.

**Why it leaks:**
A stranger arriving from a cold ad has zero reason to trust you. Their internal question is: "Did this actually work for someone like me?" If the page can't answer that question with specifics, the doubt wins. Doubt is free. Your product isn't.

**The pass condition:**
At least one piece of evidence is attributable, specific, and near the point of decision. Not "customers love us." A named person, a real number, a specific outcome.

**Quick test:**
Read every trust element on your page. For each one, ask: "Would a skeptic believe this?" Generic logos and vague praise fail this test. A quote with a name, company, and specific result passes it.

## Leak 4: Your CTA button describes the action, not the outcome

**What it looks like:**

> "Submit." "Get started." "Sign up." "Learn more." "Click here."

**Why it leaks:**
The button is the last thing before the conversion. It should answer the one remaining question: "What happens when I click this?" "Submit" describes the visitor's action. It says nothing about what they get. The moment of friction is highest right before the click — and the button is doing nothing to reduce it.

**The pass condition:**
The button label tells the visitor what they receive, not what they do.

**Better examples:**

> "Get my free audit report"
> "See what's leaking on my page"
> "Start the 48-hour fix"

**Quick test:**
Read your button label alone, out of context. Does it tell a stranger what they're getting? Or does it just describe a mechanical action?

## Leak 5: The page is doing too much

**What it looks like:**
Navigation bar with six links. Secondary CTAs. Blog post excerpts. Newsletter signup. Social media icons. A footer with 40 links. Three different offers on the same page.

**Why it leaks:**
Every element that isn't the primary CTA is a competing exit path. Each navigation link is a door out of your funnel. You paid to get them to this page. Every exit option you built is a way for that ad spend to walk out the door.

**The pass condition:**
A paid-traffic landing page has one offer. One CTA. No navigation links that go somewhere other than the checkout or the next step in the funnel.

**Quick test:**
Count the number of clickable elements on your landing page that are not your primary CTA button. Every extra link is a competing conversion.

---

## Leak 6: It breaks on mobile, silently

**What it looks like:**
The desktop version looks great. On a phone: text is tiny, the CTA button is half off screen, the headline is cut off, or the form fields require pinch-zooming.

**Why it leaks:**
Most paid traffic arrives on mobile. Your ads are being shown on phones. If the experience degrades silently on mobile — and you're only checking on your desktop — you're running ads to a broken page for the majority of your audience, and you have no idea.

**The pass condition:**
The headline is readable without zooming. The CTA button is tappable with a thumb. The form works on iOS Safari and Android Chrome. The page loads in under 3 seconds on a mid-range device.

**Quick test:**
Pull up your page on your actual phone. Not Chrome DevTools. Your phone. Go through the conversion flow. Can you complete it in under 30 seconds without frustration?

---

## Leak 7: The page answers questions nobody asked

**What it looks like:**
A 4,000-word page explaining the company's history, the technology behind the product, the founder's LinkedIn bio, the roadmap, every feature in exhaustive detail, and a FAQ with 22 questions.

**Why it leaks:**
Cold traffic visitors from an ad are not researchers. They have one question: *"Is this the right solution for the thing I'm dealing with right now?"* A page that buries the answer to that question under information they didn't ask for makes them work to justify buying. Friction increases. Confidence drops.

**The pass condition:**
The page answers the visitor's single most urgent question in the first viewport. Everything below the fold earns its place by removing a specific objection, not by adding information.

**Quick test:**
Read your page and mark every sentence as either "removes doubt" or "adds information." A conversion-optimized page is mostly "removes doubt." An explanation page is mostly "adds information." Cold traffic needs the first kind.
